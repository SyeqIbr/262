B1 Cell biology · Note 1 of 6

Animal, plant & bacterial cells

In this note you'll learn to

  • Name the parts of animal, plant and bacterial cells, and what each part does
  • Explain the difference between eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells
  • Compare sizes using orders of magnitude
  • Describe how specialised cells are adapted for their jobs

Two kinds of cell

Every living thing is made of cells. Plant and animal cells are eukaryotic: their genetic material is enclosed in a nucleus. Bacterial cells are prokaryotic: they have no nucleus. Instead, their DNA is a single loop that floats in the cytoplasm, and they may also have small extra rings of DNA called plasmids. Prokaryotic cells are much smaller than eukaryotic cells.

Orders of magnitude

Cells come in very different sizes, so scientists compare them using orders of magnitude. Each order of magnitude is a factor of 10.

Worked example

A plant cell is 100 µm wide. A bacterium is 1 µm wide. How many orders of magnitude bigger is the plant cell?

100 ÷ 1 = 100, and 100 = 10 × 10 = 102

The plant cell is 2 orders of magnitude bigger.

Specialised cells

Cells become specialised through differentiation: as a cell matures, it develops the parts it needs for a particular job. Most animal cells differentiate early in life. Many plant cells keep the ability to differentiate throughout their lives. In mature animals, cell division is mainly used for repair and replacement.

Key words

Eukaryotic
A cell whose genetic material is enclosed in a nucleus, e.g. plant and animal cells.
Prokaryotic
A cell with no nucleus, e.g. bacteria. Its DNA is a single loop in the cytoplasm.
Plasmid
A small ring of extra DNA found in bacteria.
Differentiation
The process by which a cell becomes specialised for a particular job.
Order of magnitude
A factor of 10, used to compare sizes.
